How Taiwan got here

You can’t understand this election without a little history, starting in 1949.

1949

A civil war splits China in two

After World War II, the Nationalists (KMT, 國民黨) and the Communists fought over who would rule China. In 1949 the Communists won the mainland and founded the People’s Republic of China in Beijing.

The Nationalist government, the Republic of China, moved to Taiwan with about 1 to 2 million soldiers and families. Both governments still exist today, and that is the root of the tension.

1949 – 1987 · Martial law 戒嚴

38 years without free elections

Under martial law, the army and police had special power over daily life. New parties were banned, newspapers were controlled, and people could be jailed for criticizing the government.

On July 15, 1987, martial law ended. The TV news card shown here announced it the night before: “President Chiang orders martial law lifted at midnight.”

1986 – today

Taiwan becomes a democracy

  1. 1986Activists form the DPP, the first real opposition party.
  2. 1987Martial law ends. People can protest and start newspapers.
  3. 1996Voters choose their president directly for the first time. China fires test missiles nearby, and the US sends aircraft carriers.
  4. 2000Power passes peacefully from one party to another.
  5. TodayPower has changed hands peacefully three times.

Taiwan, China and the United States

Three sides, three different views

China (PRC)

“Taiwan is part of China”

Beijing has never ruled Taiwan but says it must one day be joined to China. It has not ruled out using force, and its military often holds drills around the island.

Taiwan (ROC)

“We govern ourselves”

Taiwan has its own president, army, money, passports and elections. Most people want to keep their way of life. They disagree on how close to be with China.

United States

“Keep the peace”

Since 1979 the US officially recognizes Beijing, not Taipei. Under the Taiwan Relations Act it sells Taiwan weapons for defense and keeps close unofficial ties.

Why the world watches

The chips inside your phone, laptop and game console were very likely made in Taiwan.

Taiwan makes most of the world’s most advanced computer chips. So what happens there, including who leads its cities, matters to people everywhere.

The map

22 cities and counties

Six are giant cities called special municipalities (直轄市, in bold on the map). Almost 7 in 10 people in Taiwan live in one of them: Taipei, New Taipei and Taoyuan in the north, Taichung in the middle, and Tainan and Kaohsiung in the south.

The other 16 include small islands close to China, like Kinmen and Matsu (Lienchiang). Try finding your family’s hometown on the map.

The main parties

Blue, green and white

Blue · KMT 國民黨

Kuomintang

The oldest party. It came from China in 1949 and ruled Taiwan alone until 2000. It favors talking and trading with China to keep the peace, and opposes Taiwan independence.

Green · DPP 民進黨

Democratic Progressive Party

Born in 1986 from the democracy movement. It stresses that Taiwan is separate from the PRC and works closely with the US and Japan. President Lai Ching-te (賴清德) is DPP.

White · TPP 民眾黨

Taiwan People’s Party

Founded in 2019. It calls itself a practical middle choice between blue and green. In 2026 it is teaming up with the KMT in several races.

Also on the ballot · Referendum Case 22 公投第22案

A yes-or-no question about nuclear power

“Do you support the government using nuclear power to boost the economy, ending the ‘nuclear-free homeland’ energy policy?”

Voters mark Agree (同意) or Disagree (不同意).

Background: Taiwan switched off its last nuclear reactor in May 2025. Some people want nuclear power back because it is cheap and low-carbon. Others worry about earthquakes and nuclear waste.

To pass: more yes than no votes, and the yes votes must equal at least one quarter of all eligible voters.

Why Beijing and Washington are watching

A local vote with a big audience

  • A warm-up for 2028. The next presidential election is in 2028. Winning big cities now helps a party then.
  • A signal about China. Parties differ on how close to be with Beijing, so results are read around the world.
  • Fake news and meddling. By September 2026, investigators had opened 54 cases of suspected foreign interference or election fraud.

Taiwanese living overseas

No mail-in ballots. No voting at the embassy. To vote, you have to be there.

Americans living abroad can mail in their ballots. Taiwan has no absentee voting, so Taiwanese citizens in the US must fly home and vote in person at their own neighborhood polling station.

Chapter 3

Flying home to vote

Many Taiwanese in America spend a lot of money and time to cast one ballot. Here is how it works, and why they do it.

The rules

Before you board the plane

  • Household registration (戶籍) must still be active. Living abroad 2+ years without a trip home can get it removed.
  • 4 months: you must have been registered in that city or county for at least 4 months.
  • Age 20+ for local races, 18+ for the referendum.
  • Bring your ID card (身分證). A passport is not accepted. A personal seal is optional, and it must never touch the ballot.

The journey 返鄉投票

13 hours in the air for one ballot

  1. FriLand by Friday, Nov. 27. Flying west you cross the date line and lose a day.
  2. NightTaiwan’s high-speed rail added extra trains, even overnight ones, so voters can reach their hometowns.
  3. SatVote between 8 AM and 4 PM. Counting starts right after, in the same room, open to the public.
  4. CostA round trip can cost over $1,000, plus time off work and school.

Why it matters

Why fly across the ocean to vote?

Close races

3,254 votes

In 2018, Taipei’s mayor won by just 3,254 votes out of about 1.4 million. That is fewer people than fit in a small sports arena.

A hard-won right

Only since 1987

Your grandparents’ generation grew up without free elections. Voting honors the people who fought for this right.

A message to the world

“We decide”

When people travel thousands of miles to vote, it shows that Taiwan’s future is chosen by its own people, peacefully.

Chapter 4

What does this mean for you, as a Taiwanese American?

You live in the US and you can’t vote in Taiwan yet. This election still touches your life in more ways than you might think.

Your family story

This history is your history

Many Taiwanese families came to America in the 1960s to 1990s, some during martial law. Your parents or grandparents may remember a Taiwan with no free elections, or the first time they voted for a president. Their choices are part of why you grew up here.

Your citizenship

You may be Taiwanese too

If one of your parents is a Taiwanese citizen, you may also count as one under Taiwan’s law. Someday you could get a Taiwan passport, register a household there, and vote when you are 20. Ask your parents what your status is.

Your American vote

At 18, your voice reaches Taiwan

The US Congress decides things like the Taiwan Relations Act and weapons sales. When you turn 18, the people you vote for in America help shape how the US treats Taiwan. You can write to your representatives even before then.

Your devices and the economy

Peace in Taiwan keeps your world running

If there were a crisis in the Taiwan Strait, chips could run short, and phones, game consoles and cars could get more expensive or hard to find. A calm, stable Taiwan matters to every American family.

Your superpower: two languages, two cultures

You can explain Taiwan to your friends

Most of your classmates don’t know where Taiwan is or why it matters. You can. Taiwanese American Heritage Week is celebrated each May, which is a great time to share what you learned.

Your job this week

Be a fake-news detective

During elections, false stories spread fast in family chat groups like LINE. If a grandparent forwards something shocking, help them check it: who posted it, is it on a real news site, is the photo old?

A note for parents

A child with ROC nationality who later registers a household in Taiwan takes on the duties that come with it. For boys, that can include military service. Before registering a child, check the current rules with the nearest Taipei Economic and Cultural Office (TECO).

Talk with your family

Questions to ask at dinner

  1. Did you or our grandparents ever vote in Taiwan? What was it like?
  2. What do you remember about martial law, or the first presidential election in 1996?
  3. Am I a Taiwanese citizen too? Could I vote there one day?
  4. Why do you think Taiwan doesn’t allow voting by mail? What are the good and bad sides?
  5. If you were mayor of our family’s hometown, what would you fix first?
  6. Would you fly 13 hours to vote? Why or why not?

Fun fact

The 30-meter line

Police put up signs like this one 30 meters (about 100 feet) from every polling station. Inside that line, nobody is allowed to campaign or tell people how to vote. Breaking the rule can mean a fine or even jail.
